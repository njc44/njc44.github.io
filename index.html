Forecasting · 2025–26

Delivery-date promises on the cart

A quantile model behind the delivery date shown when you enter your pincode. Delivery breaches fell from 8% to under 5%, and it serves 4K RPS in under 15 ms.

Problem

A delivery date that's too optimistic leads to breaches, cancellations and returns. One that's too cautious loses the sale. The date has to be calculated live for every cart, within the cart's latency budget.

What I built

  • An end-to-end pipeline that builds the training target and features from carrier milestone timestamps and in-house order data.
  • Rolling statistics of each delivery leg (seller handover, line-haul, last mile) for sellers, lanes and carriers, plus calendar and sale-day signals. To keep this cheap on entities with hundreds of millions of rows, rows are first collapsed to one per entity per day. Rolling percentiles and higher moments are then computed from daily aggregates.
  • A LightGBM model trained with pinball (quantile) loss. It predicts a high quantile of delivery time instead of the mean, so the promise covers most deliveries.
  • A Triton ensemble for serving: a Python preprocessing step, then the tree model on the FIL backend, with dynamic batching. All heavy features are computed in batch beforehand, so only lightweight work happens per request.

Decisions that mattered

  • I first predicted the mean and then the residual in a second stage. The second stage collapsed to a constant offset, so a single pinball model replaced both and was simpler.
  • Rounding matters for a quantile model. Rounding up (ceil) means the rounding step can never cause a breach.
  • Features only use data that has fully landed. Mixing in partly delivered days silently biases the model.

Impact

Breaches fell from 8% to under 5%, positive MAE improved by 0.8 days, NMV rose 0.7%, cancellations fell 0.2% and RTO fell 0.1%. Next I'm working on per-leg models and a calibration layer.

NLP · small models · 2024

Intent detection for support chat and voice

I led the intent classifier work. It moved from prompting GPT-class models to a fine-tuned FLAN-T5 that is much cheaper and faster.

Problem

Every support conversation starts by working out what the customer wants. That decides which resolution procedure the bot follows. Routing errors cascade, and calling a large LLM for every turn is slow and expensive.

What I built

  • Narrowing by order state. Before any model runs, the order's status and situation (shipped, delivered, late, return in progress) cut down the set of possible intents. A small, relevant label set is much easier to classify.
  • v2 retrieval-then-rank pipeline. An LLM pulls the customer's intents out of the whole conversation. Those are embedded and matched against an intent index in a vector database to get a shortlist, and a final ranking step picks one.
  • A fine-tuned small model. A FLAN-T5-base classifier replaced the prompted LLM. It's served over gRPC, scores only the intents valid for the current order state, and falls back safely if the call fails.
  • Gradual percentage rollouts, latency logging and per-intent monitoring, so every change could be measured before it reached all traffic.

What I learned

Once the label space is constrained by order state, a small fine-tuned model can do the job of a prompted large one, at a fraction of the cost and latency.

LLMs · RAG · 2023–24

Support chatbot with conversational RAG

Retrieval over real support conversations got smaller models to GPT-4-level answers at under 25% of the cost. It beat human agents on Customer Effort Score.

Problem

A support bot has to follow the company's procedures exactly while still sounding like a person. GPT-4 did this well but cost too much at Meesho's volume. The goal was to get the same quality from smaller, cheaper models.

What I built

  • A retrieval corpus from past conversations. I mined bot and human-agent chats with Spark and replaced order-specific details (dates, products, couriers) with placeholders. The user turns were embedded and indexed in a vector database.
  • Retrieval at the right level. I tested indexing by order status, by intent, and by intent and scenario together, with a separate index for opening turns. The closest past exchanges go into the prompt as examples of tone and resolution, with instructions not to copy their specifics.
  • Grounded generation. The model answers only from the order's data and the matching procedure. A function call hands off to a human for anything outside its scope, and content-filter or error states route to an agent automatically.
  • Multi-provider routing. Traffic is weighted across several model providers and cloud regions, with circuit breakers and fallback chains. A smaller RAG model falls back to a larger one when needed.

Impact

Answers matched GPT-4 quality at under 25% of the cost, with no fine-tuning, and the bot beat human agents on Customer Effort Score. Serving optimisations with quantised models on Triton cut latency from 1500 to 40 ms. I also helped design the multilingual version, working through hallucination and coherence problems in non-English output.

Computer vision · trust & safety · 2022–25

The catalog compliance pipeline

I built the system that finds counterfeit and restricted listings. It removed 42 lakh counterfeits and cut non-compliant views by 80%, to under 0.1% of the platform.

Problem

Millions of new listings arrive from small sellers. Some carry fake brand logos or sell items that aren't allowed. Manual review couldn't keep up, and every counterfeit that slips through costs buyers' trust.

What I built

  • A staged pipeline that runs cheap checks first. Text matching with fuzzy and spelling-variant handling catches the easy cases. The rest goes through OCR, a YOLOv7 logo detector, and image embeddings compared against a logo index with GPU FAISS nearest-neighbour search. A vision-language model reviews a small set of hard cases.
  • Running it efficiently. Models load once per Spark worker and score whole partitions at a time, and OCR runs on GPUs. Only new or changed listings in relevant categories are scored, which cut OCR runtime substantially.
  • Changing platforms. I moved the pipeline from EMR and Airflow to Databricks with Delta upserts and compaction, and from S3 to GCS. Each stage was packaged as a versioned job with Grafana and Cronitor monitoring.
  • Related quality checks: image-based filters, blur detection, price-on-image detection and a profanity filter. These used custom PyTorch and TensorFlow models, PaddleOCR and multimodal models.

Impact

The pipeline removed 42 lakh counterfeit listings and deactivated 10 lakh restricted products. Non-compliant listing views fell 80%, to under 0.1% of platform views. Recall reached 99.5%, quality-check costs fell 70%, and a redesigned ETL saves about $40K/month in servers. This work was part of Meesho's publicly announced Project Suraksha.

I took batch-only catalog attribute-tagging models and turned them into autoscaling real-time services on EKS, with CI/CD.

Product-attribute models (multi-head image classifiers that fill in details sellers leave blank) only ran as batch jobs. I built the real-time serving layer for them:

  • FastAPI services with health checks that return the top value and a confidence for each attribute.

Vision

I want a world where autonomous physical agents take on much of the hard, informal, unorganised labour, so people get their time back for work that means something to them.

I believe in the dignity of labour, for everyone. That means the move to physical AI has to be designed around the people who do this work today. Automating it in the wrong economy at the wrong time takes away livelihoods.

A good guide is the Lewis turning point. That's the moment an economy runs out of surplus rural labour and wages start rising quickly. Where an economy sits on that curve should decide what physical AI does there.

Below the turning point

Countries that still have surplus labour are where physical AI should gather its training data: teleoperation, demonstrations and annotation. That work pays people and builds skills without displacing anyone.

Past the turning point

Countries with shrinking or ageing workforces are where autonomous systems should be deployed, filling gaps that people are leaving anyway.

Done in that order, physical AI adds to economies instead of hollowing them out.

Further out: quantum

I expect quantum AI applications to become practical in the 2040s. I've been reading about quantum algorithms and completed IBM's quantum computing course, so I'm ready when the hardware catches up.
